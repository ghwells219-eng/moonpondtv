Why Moon Pond Moves First, Then Breathes, Then Rests

September 28, 2026 · Gabriella Hope

Moon Pond's wind-down episodes follow three steps: move first, slow the breath, then rest. That order isn't a style choice. It's what four studies on young kids point to, and it's what 15+ years of experience coaching kids in dance and gymnastics taught us in the gym.

If bedtime at your house sometimes looks like a small tornado, you're not alone. Little bodies often need to get the wiggles out before they can settle. Here's what the research says about each step, and how to try it tonight.

1. Move first

Asking a wiggly kid to sit still and breathe usually backfires. Moving to a beat first gives that energy somewhere to go.

In a 2023 study in Early Childhood Research Quarterly, preschool teachers led 3 and 4 year olds through short sessions of rhythmic movement to music, 16 to 20 times over 8 weeks. Compared with kids who didn't get the program, those children showed gains in self-regulation: managing feelings, behavior and attention in class. The biggest growth showed up in kids from under-resourced communities.

That's why Moon Pond Party starts with Bop: shake, stomp, freeze, flop. The freeze matters too. Stopping on cue is a tiny self-control rep hidden inside a game.

2. Slow the breath

Once the wiggles are out, a few slow breaths help a body shift gears. It takes less time than you'd think.

Stanford researchers tested a one-minute animated video with 342 kids, about 7 years old on average, at museums, playgrounds and summer camps. The video guided four slow, deep breaths: smell a flower on the way in, blow out a candle on the way out. Kids who watched it had lower heart rates and higher heart rate variability, a marker of a calmer body. Kids who watched a regular informational video showed no change. The study was published in Developmental Psychobiology in 2021.

This is Luma's job. Her glow grows as you breathe in and softens as you breathe out, so kids can follow along without counting.

3. Keep the pace gentle

What kids watch right before bed matters, and so does how fast it moves.

In a 2011 study in Pediatrics, researchers Angeline Lillard and Jennifer Peterson had 4-year-olds spend nine minutes watching a fast-paced cartoon, watching a slow-paced realistic show, or drawing. Right afterward, kids who watched the fast-paced cartoon did worse on tasks that test focus, problem-solving and self-control. Kids who watched the slower show did about as well as kids who drew (summary).

So Moon Pond keeps it slow on purpose: gentle cuts, soft colors, no flashing, no shouting. Even Bop's party ends in a slow breath.

4. Same routine, every night

The routine itself does a lot of the work. Doing the same steps in the same order tells a small body what comes next.

A 2015 study led by sleep researcher Jodi Mindell surveyed 10,085 mothers in 14 countries about kids up to 6 years old. Kids with a consistent bedtime routine went to bed earlier, fell asleep faster, woke less at night and slept longer. Kids with a nightly routine slept more than an hour longer per night than kids with none. The more nights a week the routine happened, the better the results, and the pattern held across countries and cultures.

That's why Hush episodes end the same way, with Snoozle's yawn and Luma's goodnight: "And just remember... I love you to the moon and back."

Try it tonight: a 10-minute wind-down

You don't need a screen for this. Try it with your kids tonight, and keep it the same every night.

  1. Wiggle (3 minutes). Put on one upbeat song. Shake hands, then feet. Stomp, stomp, stomp. Freeze! Then flop like a floppy noodle.
  2. Breathe (2 minutes). Sit close. Smell the flower, blow out the candle. Four slow breaths, and let the out-breath be the longer one.
  3. Dream (5 minutes). Lights low. Say goodnight to toes, knees, tummy and nose. End with the same goodnight words every night.

On busy nights, a Moon Pond episode can lead the way. Our wind-down episodes follow this same order, from wiggles to dreams.
